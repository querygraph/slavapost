Today's edition is about "Open" AI models.

As you know, there is an ongoing technological and geopolitical competition between the United States and China. The U.S. government has even discussed restricting open-weight AI models developed in China, and it has recently imposed restrictions on certain Chinese robotics technologies. However, that's not the main topic I want to discuss today.

What I want to focus on is the way we use the word "open" when talking about AI models.

In my view, many of the models marketed as open cannot be described as "open". For example, some Chinese models officially release their model weights, but they contain trillions of parameters. In practice, almost nobody can run them on their own hardware. You still need access to expensive cloud infrastructure or a large data center, which means using them requires significant financial resources.

So the question is: how can these models be considered truly open?

Making the weights available for download is only one aspect of openness. What's often missing is transparency about how the model was trained, which datasets were used, what computing resources were required, and whether the training data included copyrighted material or other content with legal restrictions.

Without that level of transparency, "open" becomes a very limited concept.

From a legal perspective, there are also important questions. If you're building products or services on top of these models, who actually owns the content used to train them? Were the original creators aware that their work would become part of a commercial AI system? Were they compensated or did they give consent?

These are issues that will become increasingly important in the future.

People often talk about owning AI or owning intelligence, but in reality you're still dependent on the infrastructure behind the model. If a model can only realistically be run in large data centers, or if its license or availability can change overnight because of regulations or company decisions, then your independence is limited.

My advice is simple: be very cautious whenever a model is described as "open." Ask yourself whether it could become restricted tomorrow due to regulation, licensing changes, or infrastructure requirements.

This discussion is particularly relevant in the European Union, where new AI regulations continue to evolve. We need greater clarity about whether training data comes from legally usable public sources and whether authors have explicitly granted permission for their work to be used for AI training.

Until we have that transparency and consent, I don't believe we should automatically describe these systems as truly open.

I also think the traditional concept of open data may need to evolve. Historically, we considered publicly accessible information to be open for people to read and reuse. But that doesn't necessarily mean individuals intended for massive corporations - whether American, Chinese, or from elsewhere - to ingest that information into proprietary AI models and then sell AI-powered services back to the public.

That's one of the biggest challenges I see ahead.

If your goal is to truly own your AI infrastructure, even very large open-weight models such as Kimi K3 are not necessarily the solution. They represent an impressive technological achievement, but they're still fundamentally monolithic systems developed and controlled by a single company. In that sense, they belong to the organization that built them—not to society as a whole - even if much of their knowledge ultimately originates from publicly available sources.

### A Different European Approach

In contrast, what we're building in Europe follows a fundamentally different philosophy from both the American and Chinese approaches.

I've mentioned this several times before. Our goal is to treat data as an asset. More importantly, data should become a protected form of intellectual property.

If you produce data and you don't want to share it with someone else, that choice should belong to you. If they really want to use your data, they should first obtain your consent. That could mean purchasing a subscription, negotiating a license, signing a smart contract, or agreeing on some other legal framework.

The important principle is that access to data should be negotiated - not simply taken without your knowledge or permission.

To support this vision, we're building on decentralized identifiers (DIDs), a long-established W3C standard that has evolved over the past several years.

A decentralized identifier combines a public key and a private key. The private key allows the owner to control and update information, while the public key allows anyone to verify authorship and authenticity.

One of the strengths of this approach is that a decentralized identifier can be assigned to almost anything. It can identify a person, a company, a document, a dataset, a digital artifact, or virtually any other resource.

Once an identifier is attached, it becomes possible to maintain complete provenance information throughout the entire lifecycle of that asset.

For example, imagine someone writes information on paper. They then photograph that paper using a mobile phone. Optical character recognition (OCR) converts the image into text, and the text is transformed into a structured dataset. And we need to define how we measure something in that datasets, and make it AI-Ready.

Although the information has gone through several technical transformations, the provenance should remain connected to the original source. The original author should continue to receive attribution regardless of how many times the content is transformed, processed, or reused.

Whether the data moves between applications, organizations, or countries, we should always be able to trace it back to its original creator.

This kind of provenance is largely missing from today's AI systems.

Another challenge concerns the way current large language models are optimized. They are designed to minimize prediction error across enormous datasets, which naturally favors statistically common patterns. Less common viewpoints, minority perspectives, regional variations, and historical context can become underrepresented or effectively treated as statistical outliers.

As a result, AI systems often produce responses that represent an average or generalized view rather than preserving the diversity of perspectives that exist in reality.

They also struggle to preserve temporal and geographic context. Information originating from different countries, cultures, historical periods, or even centuries may be merged together into what appears to be a single, coherent answer, even though those pieces of information belong to very different contexts.

The result is that users may receive responses that appear authoritative but blur important distinctions between sources, places, and periods of time.

For this reason, we need to pay much closer attention to how information is structured, where it comes from, and who originally produced it. The challenge isn't limited to hallucinations. It also includes misinformation, missing context, loss of provenance, and the inability to distinguish between competing interpretations.

These are precisely the kinds of problems that we believe European digital infrastructure should address. Building trustworthy AI isn't only about creating more capable models; it's also about creating systems that preserve provenance, respect ownership, support consent, and provide transparency throughout the entire lifecycle of data.

That, in my view, represents a fundamentally different direction from the dominant approaches currently being pursued in both the United States and China.
